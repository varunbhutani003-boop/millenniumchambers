# Non-Compete Clauses in India: What Startups Can and Cannot Enforce

## Why non-competes matter to Indian startups

Almost every Indian startup has a non-compete clause somewhere: in employment letters, in consultant agreements, in the shareholders' agreement that binds the founders, or in a business-transfer agreement. Many of those clauses would not survive a court challenge in the form they are written. The reason is a single provision of a law passed in 1872.

This guide explains how Indian law treats non-compete, non-solicitation and confidentiality clauses, and how a startup can draft its contracts so that the protections it relies on are the ones a court will actually enforce. It is general information, not legal advice.

### The rule: Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872

Section 27 says that every agreement by which anyone is restrained from exercising a lawful profession, trade or business of any kind is, to that extent, void. Unlike English law, which allows restraints that are "reasonable", the Indian provision does not contain a general reasonableness test. The Supreme Court has read it strictly for more than five decades.

The section has one statutory exception: a person who sells the goodwill of a business may agree with the buyer not to carry on a similar business within specified local limits, for as long as the buyer (or anyone deriving title to the goodwill from the buyer) carries on a like business there, provided the limits are reasonable having regard to the nature of the business.

### Restraints during employment: generally enforceable

In *Niranjan Shankar Golikari v. Century Spinning and Manufacturing Co.* (1967), the Supreme Court held that a negative covenant that applies while the contract of employment is in force, such as a promise not to work for anyone else during the term, is not a restraint of trade under Section 27. The same reasoning was applied to commercial agreements in *Gujarat Bottling Co. v. Coca Cola Co.* (1995), where a covenant operating during the term of a franchise agreement was upheld.

For a startup, this means an employee can be required to work exclusively for the company, and not to run a competing side business, while employed. A garden-leave clause, where the employee stays on the payroll during the notice period but is kept away from work and clients, rests on the same principle because the employment continues.

### Restraints after employment ends: generally void

The position changes once the relationship ends. In *Superintendence Company of India v. Krishan Murgai* (1980), the Supreme Court held that a covenant restraining a former employee from carrying on a similar business after leaving was void under Section 27. In *Percept D'Mark (India) v. Zaheer Khan* (2006), it held that a post-term right of first refusal in a management contract was also a restraint and void.

In practice, a clause that bars an employee from joining a competitor for one or two years after resigning is very unlikely to be enforced against that employee, however carefully its duration or territory is limited. Courts have also declined injunctions against former employees who simply joined a rival (for example, *American Express Bank v. Priya Puri*, Delhi High Court, 2006).

### Non-solicitation clauses: a mixed picture

Clauses that stop a departing employee from soliciting the company's staff or customers sit in a grey zone. Some decisions have upheld covenants not to poach employees, treating them as outside Section 27 (for example, *Wipro Ltd. v. Beckman Coulter International*, Delhi High Court, 2006). Others have refused to enforce broad customer non-solicitation covenants after employment, treating them as a restraint on trade in substance. A narrowly drafted clause tied to the company's confidential customer information stands on firmer ground than a blanket ban on dealing with any customer.

### What is reliably protected: confidential information and IP

Section 27 does not stop a company from protecting its confidential information and trade secrets. Courts in India regularly restrain former employees from using or disclosing client lists, pricing data, source code, product roadmaps and similar information, whether through the contract or through the equitable duty of confidence. Clear intellectual-property assignment clauses, under which work created in the course of employment belongs to the company, are equally important and are not affected by Section 27.

For most startups, a well-drafted confidentiality and IP clause, supported by access controls and exit procedures, does more real work than a post-employment non-compete.

### Founders and shareholders' agreements

Investors usually ask founders to agree not to compete while they are shareholders or employees and for a period afterwards. While the founder remains in the business, the Golikari line of cases supports the restraint. After exit, a founder non-compete is easier to defend where it is linked to a sale of shares and the goodwill of the business, which brings it closer to the statutory exception, than where it is a free-standing ban. Founders should read this clause carefully before signing a term sheet; see our guides on [term sheets](/term-sheet-startups-india) and [founders' agreements](/founders-agreement-startups-india).

### A drafting checklist for startups

- Keep exclusivity and no-moonlighting obligations limited to the term of employment, including any notice period.
- Use garden leave, rather than a post-exit non-compete, where you need to keep a senior employee away from clients for a few weeks.
- Define confidential information specifically, and make the duty survive termination.
- Include a present assignment of intellectual property created in the course of work.
- Draft non-solicitation narrowly, by reference to staff and customers the person actually dealt with.
- In share-purchase or business-transfer deals, tie the seller's non-compete to the goodwill being sold, with a defined territory and duration.
- Run an exit process: return of devices and documents, revocation of access, and a written reminder of continuing obligations.

### Common questions

**Is a non-compete clause enforceable in India after an employee resigns?**
Generally no. Under Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act and Supreme Court decisions such as Superintendence Company of India v. Krishan Murgai (1980), a restraint that operates after employment ends is void, even if it is limited in time or territory.

**Can a startup stop an employee from working for a competitor during the notice period?**
Usually yes. A restraint that operates while the employment subsists, including a paid notice or garden-leave period, is not treated as a restraint of trade under Section 27.

**Are non-solicitation clauses valid in India?**
Courts have taken different views. Covenants not to poach employees have been upheld in some cases, while broad post-employment bans on dealing with customers have been refused. Narrow drafting linked to confidential information is safer.

**Can a former employee be stopped from using our client list?**
Yes. Confidential information and trade secrets can be protected by injunction, independent of any non-compete clause.

**Do the new Labour Codes change the position on non-competes?**
No. The four Labour Codes deal with wages, social security, industrial relations and working conditions; enforceability of restrictive covenants is still governed by Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act. See our [Labour Codes guide for startups](/labour-codes-startups-india-guide).
