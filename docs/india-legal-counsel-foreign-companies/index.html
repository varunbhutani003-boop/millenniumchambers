India Legal Counsel for Foreign Companies and Law Firms

Recovery, compliance, licences and India-side legal support for businesses outside India

Millennium Chambers, the practice of Advocate Varun Bhutani at 325H, Sector 38, Gurugram (Gurgaon), works with companies, investors and law firms based outside India that have a matter in India: money owed by an Indian buyer, a product or service being sold to Indian customers, an Indian entity or office to set up, or a contract with an Indian counterparty. Varun has spent close to eight years inside Indian companies, most recently as Head of Legal, and before that on the founding legal team of an NBFC where he ran recovery portfolios across courts and tribunals. The same work is now done for clients abroad who need someone on the ground in India.

Advice on Indian law and appearance before Indian courts and tribunals is reserved to advocates enrolled in India. This practice acts as India-side counsel and works alongside the client's own lawyers in their home country.

Who this is for

  • Exporters and service providers abroad with unpaid invoices from Indian buyers, distributors or agents.

  • Foreign companies selling into India through a website, an app, a marketplace or a distributor, without an Indian entity of their own.

  • Companies setting up in India: a subsidiary, a branch, liaison or project office, a franchise or a joint venture.

  • Law firms and consultancies outside India that need Indian-law input, local filings or representation for their own clients.

  • Indian-origin founders abroad with a business, investment or dispute in India.

Recovering money from Indian parties

  • Demand notice and settlement. A notice from an Indian advocate, drawn on the contract and the paper trail, is the usual first step and settles a fair share of claims. It also fixes the amount and the date from which limitation and interest are counted.

  • Suing in India. A foreign company can sue in Indian courts in its own name. Claims on written contracts and invoices can go as summary suits under Order XXXVII of the Code of Civil Procedure, and commercial disputes of Rs 3 lakh and above go to the Commercial Courts, where pre-institution mediation is mandatory unless urgent interim relief is sought.

  • Enforcing a foreign court judgment. A money decree from a superior court of a "reciprocating territory" notified under Section 44A of the Code of Civil Procedure can be executed in India as if an Indian district court had passed it. The United Kingdom, Singapore, Hong Kong and, since January 2020, the United Arab Emirates are among the notified territories. A judgment from any other country, including the United States, is enforced by filing a fresh suit in India on the foreign judgment. In both cases the judgment must meet the tests in Section 13, such as being on the merits and passed with jurisdiction and notice.

  • Enforcing a foreign arbitral award. India is a party to the New York Convention. An award made in a Convention country that India has notified is enforced under Part II of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 before the High Court, on limited grounds of refusal. Indian courts can also grant interim protection, such as securing the amount in dispute, in support of an arbitration seated abroad unless the parties have agreed otherwise.

  • Insolvency proceedings. A foreign creditor has the same standing as an Indian one under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code. Where the default is Rs 1 crore or more, an application can be made to the NCLT, and claims can be filed in a corporate insolvency or liquidation already under way.

  • Cheque dishonour and criminal complaints. Where an Indian party's cheque is returned unpaid, or the facts show cheating and not only non-payment, a complaint can run alongside the civil claim.

Compliance for businesses dealing with India

  • Foreign investment and exchange control. The FEMA rules on foreign direct investment: sectoral caps, the automatic and government-approval routes, pricing and reporting of share issues and transfers, and the revised 2026 rules for investors connected with countries sharing a land border with India.

  • Data protection. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 applies to processing outside India where it is connected with offering goods or services to people in India. See the DPDP guide.

  • Consumer and e-commerce law. Indian-law terms of sale, refund and grievance policies, country-of-origin and seller disclosures, advertising claims and dark-pattern rules for sites and apps that sell to Indian consumers.

  • Product rules. Labelling and declarations for imported packaged goods, food and supplement labelling and claims under FSSAI regulations, quality-control orders and standards marks, and plastic-packaging obligations for importers and brand owners.

  • Employment and contractors. Hiring in India directly, through an employer-of-record or as contractors: the Labour Codes, mandatory policies, and the risk of contractors being treated as employees. See the Labour Codes guide.

  • Corporate upkeep. Board, filing and record requirements for an Indian subsidiary, and the annual activity certificate and filings for a branch or liaison office.

  • Compliance review. A review of a foreign company's India-facing website, contracts and listings against Indian law, with a written list of gaps in order of risk.

Licences, registrations and entry into India

  • Choosing the vehicle. A wholly owned subsidiary, an LLP, a joint venture, or a branch, liaison or project office. Offices of a foreign company are set up under the FEMA regulations of 2016 through an authorised dealer bank, with prior Reserve Bank approval in specified cases, and are then registered with the Registrar of Companies.

  • Business registrations. Company incorporation, PAN and TAN, GST, Importer-Exporter Code and the state shops and establishments registration. See business registrations and licences in India.

  • Sector licences. FSSAI licences for food importers and brands, importer registration for packaged commodities under the Legal Metrology rules, BIS certification where a product is covered, drug and cosmetic import registrations, and producer-responsibility registrations for packaging and e-waste.

  • Trademarks. Filing in India directly or designating India through the Madrid Protocol, replies to examination reports, oppositions and action against copycat marks. See the trademark guide.

  • Property and premises. Office leases and leave-and-licence agreements, and the FEMA limits on what a foreign company or its Indian office may acquire.

Other legal services

  • Contracts with Indian counterparties. Distribution, agency, franchise, supply, technology and services agreements, with governing law, arbitration seat and payment security chosen so that the contract can be enforced against the Indian party.

  • Checks before a deal. Company, litigation, charge and trademark searches on an Indian buyer, distributor, franchisee or target.

  • Replies to Indian notices. Legal notices, consumer complaints and regulator letters addressed to a foreign company. See replying to a legal notice.

  • Ongoing counsel. An India legal desk on a monthly arrangement for companies with regular Indian work. See legal retainer.

  • Support to foreign law firms. Indian-law notes, local filings, searches, and conduct of Indian proceedings on instructions from the client's lawyers abroad.

Questions foreign companies ask

Can a foreign company sue an Indian company in India? Yes. A company incorporated outside India can file a suit, an arbitration-related petition or an insolvency application in India in its own name, through an authorised signatory holding a board resolution or power of attorney. Documents signed abroad are usually notarised and apostilled or consularised before use in India.

Is a foreign court judgment enforceable in India? A money decree from a superior court of a reciprocating territory notified under Section 44A of the Code of Civil Procedure can be executed directly. A judgment from any other country needs a fresh suit in India based on that judgment. In both cases the Indian court checks the conditions in Section 13 of the Code.

How long does a foreign creditor have to recover a debt in India? For most money claims the period is three years from the date the amount became due. A written acknowledgment of the debt or a part payment within that period starts a fresh period, so the correspondence with the Indian party matters.

Does a foreign company need an Indian entity to sell to customers in India? Not always. Many foreign businesses sell through a distributor, a marketplace or directly online. Indian consumer, data protection, tax and product rules can still apply to those sales, and some licences can only be held by an Indian entity or importer.

Can the work be done without travelling to India? In most matters, yes. Instructions, documents and signatures are handled remotely, and court and tribunal work is taken in Gurugram and Delhi NCR. Matters in other Indian cities are run with local counsel.
