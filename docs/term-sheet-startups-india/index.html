Term Sheets for Startups in India

By Varun Bhutani, Advocate · Millennium Chambers, Gurugram · Updated 12 November 2026

Term sheets in India: the clauses founders should understand

A term sheet sets out the main commercial terms on which an investor proposes to invest in a startup. It is usually short, and most of its terms are expressly non-binding: they are later written into a share subscription agreement (SSA), a shareholders' agreement (SHA) and the company's amended Articles of Association. This note by Advocate Varun Bhutani of Millennium Chambers, Gurugram, explains the clauses Indian founders most often negotiate. It is general information only, not legal advice.

1. Valuation, instrument and the ESOP pool

The term sheet states the pre-money valuation, the amount being invested and the security to be issued. Indian venture rounds commonly use compulsorily convertible preference shares (CCPS), which convert into equity shares on agreed terms. Check whether the price is worked out on a fully diluted basis and whether a new or larger ESOP pool must be created before the round, because a pre-money pool dilutes the existing shareholders rather than the incoming investor.

2. Liquidation preference

A liquidation preference decides who is paid first, and how much, if the company is sold or wound up. Under a "1x non-participating" preference, the investor receives either the amount it invested or what it would get by converting and sharing pro rata, whichever is higher. A multiple above 1x, or a "participating" preference that pays the investor first and then lets it share in the balance as well, is more investor-friendly and reduces what founders receive on an exit.

3. Anti-dilution protection

If the company later raises money at a lower price (a "down round"), an anti-dilution clause adjusts the investor's conversion price. A broad-based weighted average adjustment is the common market position. Full ratchet protection, which resets the conversion price to the new lower price, is much harsher on founders.

4. Board seats and investor consent rights

Investors often ask for a board seat or an observer seat and a list of "reserved matters" that need their consent, such as changing the line of business, issuing new shares, taking on large debt, related-party transactions or amending the Articles. A focused list keeps day-to-day decisions with the founders and the management team.

5. Founder vesting, transfers and exit rights

Term sheets usually include founder lock-in or reverse vesting, a right of first refusal on share transfers, tag-along and drag-along rights, and pre-emptive (pro rata) rights in future rounds. Some also set a period within which the investor expects an exit through an IPO or a strategic sale. These terms build on what the founders have already agreed among themselves.

6. The binding clauses: exclusivity, confidentiality and costs

Even a non-binding term sheet normally makes a few clauses binding: exclusivity (a "no-shop" period during which the founders cannot negotiate with other investors), confidentiality, governing law and, in some cases, which side bears legal and due-diligence costs. The length of the exclusivity period deserves attention.

Indian law steps that follow a signed term sheet

Under the Companies Act, 2013, a private company issuing shares to an investor usually does so as a preferential allotment under Section 62(1)(c), which needs a special resolution of the shareholders and a valuation report from a registered valuer, and as a private placement under Section 42, which uses a private placement offer letter in Form PAS-4 and requires the subscription money to come from the investor's own bank account, not in cash. Where the investor is outside India, the issue must also comply with the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-Debt Instruments) Rules, 2019, including the pricing guidelines, and be reported to the Reserve Bank of India on its FIRMS portal. The "angel tax" under Section 56(2)(viib) of the Income-tax Act, 1961 was abolished by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 with effect from assessment year 2025-26.

Term sheet checklist for founders

  • Pre-money valuation, instrument (often CCPS) and fully diluted basis understood

  • Size and timing of any ESOP pool top-up agreed

  • Liquidation preference multiple and participation checked

  • Anti-dilution on a weighted average basis rather than full ratchet

  • Board composition and reserved matters kept focused

  • Founder vesting, right of first refusal, tag-along, drag-along and exit timelines reviewed

  • Exclusivity period, confidentiality and costs clauses read carefully

  • Companies Act and FEMA steps planned: special resolution, valuation report, Form PAS-4 and, for foreign investment, FIRMS reporting
