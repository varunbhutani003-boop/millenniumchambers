Cheque Bounce Under Section 138: A Guide for Businesses Owed Money

By Varun Bhutani, Advocate · Millennium Chambers, Gurugram · Updated 20 November 2026

When a customer's cheque is returned unpaid

For many small and growing businesses, a post-dated cheque is the only security they hold for a customer's dues. When that cheque comes back from the bank marked "funds insufficient" or "exceeds arrangement", Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 gives the business a criminal remedy that is often faster and more persuasive than a civil recovery suit. It works only if a short series of deadlines is met exactly. Most cheque-bounce complaints that fail do so on timing, not on the merits.

This guide sets out how the process works for a business on the receiving end of a dishonoured cheque. It is general information, not legal advice.

What has to be true for Section 138 to apply

  • The cheque was given for a legally enforceable debt or liability. A cheque issued as a gift or as a pure security deposit with no debt due can be contested. Invoices, a ledger, a purchase order or a loan agreement show the debt behind the cheque.

  • It was presented within its validity. Cheques are valid for three months from the date written on them.

  • It was returned unpaid for insufficient funds, for exceeding the arrangement, or for reasons the courts treat the same way, such as "account closed" or "payment stopped by drawer" where the account lacked funds.

The deadlines that decide the case

  1. Legal notice within 30 days of receiving the bank's return memo. The notice must be in writing and must demand payment of the cheque amount.

  2. The drawer has 15 days from receiving the notice to pay. If they pay within this window, no offence is made out.

  3. Complaint within one month after the 15 days run out, under Section 142(1)(b). A court can condone a short delay if sufficient cause is shown, but no one should plan around that.

Keep the return memo, the original cheque, the postal receipts and tracking printouts, and the notice itself. These are the core of the complaint.

Where to file

Since the 2015 amendment, Section 142(2) places jurisdiction with the court within whose local limits the payee's bank branch sits, for a cheque deposited into the payee's account. For a Gurugram business banking in Gurugram, that is usually the Gurugram courts, even if the customer is in another state.

What the complaint can achieve

  • Punishment of up to two years' imprisonment, or a fine of up to twice the cheque amount, or both.

  • Interim compensation under Section 143A: the court may order the accused to pay up to 20% of the cheque amount while the case is pending.

  • A deposit on appeal under Section 148: if the accused is convicted and appeals, the appellate court can require a deposit of at least 20% of the fine or compensation.

  • Compounding under Section 147: the parties can settle at any stage, and many cases end in a structured settlement once the accused has appeared.

In practice, the value of a Section 138 case for a business is often the pressure it creates to settle. A clean paper trail and a notice that leaves no room for technical objections make that pressure real.

Mistakes that sink cheque-bounce cases

  • Sending the notice on day 31 or later, or to an old address without checking the customer's current one.

  • Demanding a different amount from the cheque amount without making clear what the cheque covers.

  • Filing the complaint before the 15-day payment window has ended, or more than a month after it.

  • Not naming the company as an accused when the cheque was issued by a company, and not identifying the directors in charge of its business under Section 141.

  • No proof of the underlying debt, leaving room for the defence that the cheque was only a security.

Common questions

Can a business file a cheque-bounce case against a company? Yes. The company is the accused, and under Section 141 the persons in charge of and responsible for its business when the offence was committed, typically named directors, can also be prosecuted. The complaint must explain their role.

Is a cheque given as security covered by Section 138? It can be, if a debt was legally due on the date the cheque was presented. The courts look at whether the liability had crystallised by then, which is why invoices and ledgers matter.

Can I file a civil recovery suit as well? Yes. A Section 138 complaint and a civil suit for recovery, or a summary suit under Order XXXVII of the Code of Civil Procedure, can run side by side, since one is criminal and the other civil.

What if my business is a registered micro or small enterprise? The MSMED Act gives a separate route against buyers who pay late, through the MSE Facilitation Council, with interest at three times the bank rate. See MSME delayed-payment recovery under the MSMED Act.
